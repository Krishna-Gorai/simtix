\begin{abstract}
We present SIMTiX, a compact Single-Instruction Multiple-Thread (SIMT)
accelerator for RISC-V whose complete path---RTL, simulation, synthesis,
place-and-route, and post-route gate-level sign-off---is carried out on a single
workstation. Eight lanes grouped into four resident warps attach to a five-stage
RISC-V host through a memory-mapped offload interface and implement the full SIMT
mechanism set: warp scheduling with a background memory engine, address
coalescing, a per-warp reconvergence stack, and an on-chip scratchpad. Placing the
vector register file and the scratchpad in distributed RAM reduces LUT usage by
$5.7\times$ and closes 100~MHz timing. Every lane carries a pipelined
floating-point unit covering FP32 and FP16 add, multiply, fused multiply-add, and
iterative divide and square root, verified bit-exact against an IEEE-754 reference
over $116{,}089$ vectors; half precision reuses the single-precision datapath
through NaN-boxed widen--compute--narrow at no cycle cost. The central result is a
\emph{placed} timing-closure study of this datapath on a Zynq UltraScale+ FPGA.
The floating-point fabric is interconnect-bound: we diagnose the limit, confirm it
by de-congestion, localize it to a single lane's fused-multiply-add cone, and
close 100~MHz with a three-stage unit that moves the critical path off the
floating-point datapath. The complete system-on-chip signs off at 103.7~MHz as a
deployable, pin-constrained bitstream with a warning-free design-rule check.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
SIMT, GPGPU, RISC-V, hardware accelerator, floating-point, half precision,

%==============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================
Graphics processing units execute thousands of scalar threads in lockstep groups
called \emph{warps} under a Single-Instruction Multiple-Thread (SIMT) model. The
mechanisms that make this efficient---warp scheduling for latency hiding, memory
coalescing, control-divergence handling, and on-chip scratchpads---are well
documented for commercial GPUs~\cite{hennessy,aamodt}, but what they cost once
placed and routed is not. Production GPUs are proprietary; open GPGPU cores such
as Vortex~\cite{vortex} are powerful but large, and report pre-placement
synthesis estimates; and small academic cores are often simulator-only or stop
short of a real implementation flow. The result is that the physical behavior of
the SIMT mechanism set---which structure actually bounds frequency, and why---is
largely unreported.

This paper presents SIMTiX, a deliberately compact SIMT accelerator for RISC-V
whose entire path, from RTL through bitstream and post-route gate-level
sign-off, is carried out on a single workstation with free or widely available
tools. Compactness here is a method rather than a goal. A design small enough to
re-place and re-route in full, in hours, is one whose frequency limit can be
attributed to a named structure and then \emph{tested} by changing only that
structure and re-running the flow---a controlled experiment that is impractical
on a large core and unavailable in a simulator. SIMTiX implements the complete
SIMT mechanism set, and every lane is extended with a pipelined floating-point
unit. Its headline result is a \emph{placed} timing-closure study showing that,
once the lanes carry floating point, the design becomes interconnect-bound
rather than logic-bound, and that 100~MHz is reached by attacking the per-lane
fused multiply-add (FMA) microarchitecture, a lever identified through a
measured diagnose--confirm--localize sequence.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A SIMT accelerator (eight lanes, four resident warps) that implements the
complete mechanism set---warp scheduling, coalescing, a reconvergence stack, and
a scratchpad---in a design compact enough to carry through the entire
RTL-to-bitstream flow, integrated with a five-stage RISC-V host as a
self-contained system-on-chip (Sections~\ref{sec:arch}--\ref{sec:impl}).
\item An implementation result showing that moving the vector register file and
the scratchpad into distributed RAM reduces LUT usage by $5.7\times$ and closes
100~MHz timing, with an analysis of why the register-file read multiplexer, not
the ALUs, dominates a naive SIMT datapath (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
\item A per-lane floating-point extension covering FP32 and FP16, in which half
precision is obtained from the single-precision path via NaN-boxed
widen--compute--narrow and shown to be correctly single-rounded, verified
bit-exact over $116{,}089$ vectors (Section~\ref{sec:fp}).
\item A placed FPGA timing-closure study that diagnoses the floating-point design
as interconnect-bound, confirms the diagnosis by de-congestion, localizes the
limit to a single lane's FMA cone, closes 100~MHz with a three-stage FMA, and
carries the closure to the complete system-on-chip at 103.7~MHz with full pin
constraints and a warning-free design-rule check (Section~\ref{sec:fppa}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Architecture and Platform}\label{sec:arch}
%==============================================================================
A SIMT machine groups scalar \emph{threads} into \emph{warps} that share one
instruction stream and execute on a vector of \emph{lanes}. SIMTiX uses a warp
size equal to its lane count ($\mathrm{WARP\_SIZE}=\mathrm{NUM\_LANES}=8$) with
$\mathrm{NUM\_WARPS}=4$ warps resident, time-sharing a single
fetch/decode/execute datapath; when one warp stalls on memory, the scheduler
issues another and hides the latency. Unlike a packed-SIMD vector unit such as
the RISC-V ``V'' extension~\cite{riscvvector}, where one instruction operates on
a register-resident vector, SIMTiX keeps a scalar thread per lane with an
independent program-counter abstraction managed by the reconvergence stack, so
data-dependent control flow is handled in hardware rather than by masking in
software.

\textbf{Offload model.} The host programs a command block over a memory-mapped
(MMIO) interface---kernel PC, base pointers, and thread count $N$---writes a
\texttt{GO} bit, and polls \texttt{DONE}. On \texttt{GO} the accelerator launches
$\lceil N/8\rceil$ warps, seeding each thread's identifier and arguments through
registers at spawn. Four mechanisms make up the platform.

\textbf{Warp pool and scheduler.} Four resident slots, each with its own PC,
active mask, register-file partition, and reconvergence stack, share one datapath
and one data port. A round-robin scheduler issues one runnable warp per cycle, and
a background memory engine services a memory access while other warps' compute
instructions keep the datapath busy.

\textbf{Coalescing.} The data port is one cache line wide
($\mathrm{LINE\_WORDS}=8$, 256~bits). The memory engine groups the active lanes'
addresses by line and spends one transaction per distinct line, so a contiguous
warp access collapses to a single transaction and a fully scattered one costs up
to eight.

\textbf{Reconvergence stack.} Each warp owns a SIMT stack whose top-of-stack frame
defines the live PC and active mask. A branch on which active lanes disagree
pushes a frame so the two lane groups run in turn and reconverge at a join PC,
supporting compiler-free single-sided \texttt{if} statements and divergent loops.

\textbf{Scratchpad.} A per-warp 64-word aperture serviced from on-chip RAM lets
data reused across a warp's lanes be staged once, cutting global traffic.

The active mask additionally drives a per-lane clock enable, so masked-off lanes
are not clocked; the unused-lane fraction is dynamic energy that a
divergence-aware design saves. From lane utilization measured in our own RTL
counters, under a first-order equal-energy-per-lane-instruction model in the
spirit of activity-based GPU power models~\cite{gpuwattch}, that saving reaches
22.5\% of lane-datapath dynamic energy on a heavily divergent kernel. Every mechanism is instrumented with hardware counters
and is therefore measured directly in RTL rather than estimated in a software
model~\cite{gpgpusim}.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
%==============================================================================
SIMTiX is written in synthesizable SystemVerilog and integrated with a reused
five-stage RV32I host into a self-contained chip (\texttt{chip\_top},
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) comprising the host core, a driver ROM, the accelerator, and
an eight-bank on-chip shared memory. The host loads operands into shared memory,
launches the accelerator, and reads results back, so the entire
CPU$\rightarrow$memory$\rightarrow$accelerator$\rightarrow$memory$\rightarrow$CPU
loop runs in hardware.

\textbf{From flip-flops to LUTRAM.} The first synthesis of the accelerator on a
Zynq UltraScale+ (\texttt{xczu7ev}) consumed $170{,}868$ LUTs, 74\% of the device,
at only 10\% of its flip-flops. The cause is structural: a flip-flop vector
register file (VRF) of $4\times8\times32$ 32-bit words is $32{,}768$ registers
read as \texttt{vrf[warp][lane][reg]} with \emph{both} the warp and the register
index variable, that is, a 128-to-1 multiplexer per lane per read port. That
multiplexer fabric, not the ALUs, dominates area and sets the critical path.
Re-expressing the VRF as one distributed-RAM bank per lane, and likewise the
scratchpad, removes essentially all of those registers: LUTs fall $5.7\times$ to
$30{,}088$ (13\%), flip-flops to $4{,}590$ (1.0\%), and the design closes 100~MHz
timing with positive slack at 0.81~W. The complete chip, taken through
opt/place/phys-opt/route to a bitstream, meets all timing at 100~MHz and uses
$34{,}850$ LUTs (15.1\%), $6{,}130$ flip-flops, 24 DSP blocks, and zero block RAM
at 1.09~W; the accelerator accounts for roughly 87\% of the logic.

\textbf{Verification ladder.} Correctness is established at four levels:
cycle-accurate RTL simulation with self-checking testbenches
(Verilator~\cite{verilator}); behavioral simulation in the vendor GUI; full
place-and-route with post-route timing sign-off; and post-implementation
gate-level functional simulation of the routed netlist (UNISIM primitives). The
benchmark suite below adds a fifth, breadth-oriented level.

\section{Platform Evaluation}\label{sec:results}
%==============================================================================
The integer platform is characterized with six kernels chosen to stress distinct
mechanisms: \texttt{vadd} ($C_i=A_i+B_i$, streaming), \texttt{saxpy}
($3A_i+B_i$, coalescing), \texttt{fir} ($A_i+2A_{i+1}+A_{i+2}$, stencil),
\texttt{relu} (light divergence), \texttt{collatz} (heavy data-dependent
divergence), and \texttt{reduce} (scratchpad reduction). Each is assembled from
RISC-V source with a stock toolchain, run at $N\in\{8,64,256,1024\}$ threads, and
checked bit-exact against a golden model. For an apples-to-apples speedup, every
kernel is also written as a scalar loop and run on the same five-stage host in
RTL.

Table~\ref{tab:speedup} gives the headline platform result. Throughput ramps with
occupancy through latency hiding and saturates near 6.7 work-items per cycle,
below the eight-lane ideal because of the single shared data port. Streaming
kernels reach $7$--$10\times$ over the scalar host; \texttt{fir} is lower
($6.99\times$) because its stencil coalesces only partially; \texttt{collatz}
achieves $2.76\times$ as heavy divergence erodes, but does not eliminate, the SIMT
advantage; and \texttt{reduce} falls below break-even at $0.72\times$, since a
single-lane collapse on a single-ported scratchpad is slower than the scalar core
and calls for a dedicated cross-lane reduction primitive. SIMTiX is parameterized
in its lane and warp counts: a sweep over
$\mathrm{NUM\_LANES}\in\{4,8,16,32\}$ and $\mathrm{NUM\_WARPS}\in\{2,4,8\}$, in
which all twelve configurations elaborate and verify bit-exact, shows throughput
scaling near-linearly with lanes ($\sim\!1.97\times$ per doubling), while
occupancy beyond two warps pays off only under realistic multi-cycle memory, not
under the single-cycle behavioral model used here.

\section{Floating-Point Extension}\label{sec:fp}
%==============================================================================
The base engine is integer-only. It is extended with floating point in both
single (FP32) and half (FP16) precision while keeping the same SIMT structure:
the extension is a per-lane execute unit and a second per-lane register file, not
a new pipeline. Each lane gains a compact fixed-latency FP unit beside its
integer ALU, and a second per-lane register file holds the 32 floating-point
registers in distributed RAM, addressed by \{warp, register\} exactly like the
integer VRF, with an independent write port so that an FP result and an integer
load can retire together. The unit implements add, subtract, multiply, the fused
multiply-add in all four sign variants, sign-injection, min/max, the ordered
compares, integer$\leftrightarrow$float and FP32$\leftrightarrow$FP16 conversion,
the bit-moves, and classify, in both precisions. Rounding is round-to-nearest-even
per IEEE~754~\cite{ieee754} and subnormals are flushed to zero, a
throughput-oriented choice that keeps each lane small.

\textbf{Half precision via widen--compute--narrow.} A 16-bit half lives in a
32-bit register NaN-boxed, with the upper 16 bits all ones; a mis-boxed value
reads as a canonical NaN per the RISC-V convention~\cite{riscv}. For an FP16
operation the unit widens the operand exactly to FP32, runs the single-precision
datapath, and rounds back to FP16. This is correct rather than approximate:
because the FP32 intermediate carries a 24-bit significand and $24\ge 2\cdot11+2$,
rounding first to FP32 and then to FP16 equals rounding directly to
FP16~\cite{figueroa}, so FP16 add, subtract, multiply, the fused multiply-add, and
the conversions are correctly single-rounded. Half precision therefore reuses the
verified single-precision logic almost entirely, adding only the widen, narrow,
and box steps and, as Fig.~\ref{fig:fpthr} shows, no extra cycles.

\textbf{Fused multiply-add.} The FMA computes $\pm(a\cdot b)\pm c$ with a single
rounding~\cite{muller}, so it cannot be built by chaining the multiplier and the
adder. The unit forms the exact 48-bit product, anchors it in a wide fixed-point
accumulator, aligns the addend while capturing a sticky bit, and normalizes and
rounds once. When the addend out-ranges the product beyond the rounding window,
the product is provably below half a unit in the last place, which yields the
addend exactly and bounds the alignment shifter. The FMA has fixed latency and
commits through the ordinary writeback path; Section~\ref{sec:fppa} describes how
this combinational chain is pipelined for timing closure.

\textbf{Multi-cycle divide and square root.} Division and square root produce one
quotient or root bit per cycle by a digit recurrence~\cite{ercegovac} and are the
engine's only multi-cycle operations. Because such a core is comparatively large,
the engine has a single shared instance: when a warp issues a divide or a square
root, the engine captures all of its lanes' operands and parks the warp on a stall
scoreboard, then sequences the active lanes through the shared core one at a time.
This is the same park-and-resume mechanism the memory engine uses, so the
scheduler keeps issuing other warps onto the idle datapath meanwhile. At most one
warp's divide or square root is in flight, trading latency ($k$ active lanes take
about $k$ times as long, hidden across warps) for the area saving quantified in
Section~\ref{sec:fppa}.

\textbf{Verification.} Each unit is verified standalone against a DPI-C IEEE-754
reference: FP32 against the host's correctly-rounded \texttt{float} operations,
including \texttt{fmaf} and \texttt{sqrtf}, and FP16 against a
\texttt{double}-based reference cross-checked with \texttt{\_Float16}. Over random
vectors spanning the product-dominant, balanced (cancellation), and
addend-dominant regimes, plus directed infinity, NaN, zero, flush, and overflow
corners together with divide-by-zero, square-root-of-negative, mis-boxed-operand,
and conversion cases, the pipelined unit passes $96{,}066$ checks and the
iterative divide/square-root unit a further $20{,}023$, all bit-exact
(Table~\ref{tab:fpverif}). End-to-end FP kernels then exercise the full warp pool:
streaming add and multiply-add in each precision, a fused $C_i=A_iB_i+C_i$, and
$C_i=\sqrt{A_i/B_i}$ with two dependent multi-cycle operations per thread, all
bit-exact to 1024 threads.

\textbf{Throughput and application kernels.} The FP unit is pipelined
(Section~\ref{sec:fppa}); an FP instruction commits a fixed number of cycles after
issue and the issuing warp parks a single slot until it does, a latency that SIMT
interleaving hides on streaming work. Fig.~\ref{fig:fpthr} plots throughput
against problem size for streaming add and multiply-add in both precisions. The
latency-hiding ramp matches the integer suite and saturates near 7 work-items per
cycle at 100\% lane utilization, with the FP16 points lying exactly on the FP32
curves because half precision shares the pipelined datapath.
Table~\ref{tab:fpkernels} runs four toolchain-assembled FP kernels through the
engine. Two effects stand out. The iterative divide/square-root unit is visible:
\texttt{fnorm} $=A/\sqrt{B}$ takes about $6\times$ the cycles of streaming
\texttt{fsaxpy}, the cost of correctly-rounded iterative operations. Divergence
also interacts with the now-larger lane datapath: \texttt{fdot}'s reduction leaves
one of eight lanes active during the collapse, at 45\% utilization, so the
absolute energy a mask-gated design saves grows with the FP-enlarged lane.

($2.5\times$), flip-flops from $4{,}590$ to $7{,}894$, and DSP blocks from 24 to

\textbf{Step 1: two-stage FMA, 78~MHz.} The FP unit is first pipelined into two
stages, split at the FMA accumulator (\texttt{mag}) boundary. The warp pool
absorbs the extra cycle with a single-slot scoreboard (\texttt{W\_FPC}), the same
park-and-resume mechanism used for memory and the SFU, so no new structural hazard
arises and SIMT interleaving hides the latency. Timing-driven synthesis with
retiming cuts the FP logic depth to 5.3~ns, yet place-and-route closes at 12.8~ns
(78~MHz). The placed critical path is still the first FMA stage but is now
routing-dominated, with 7.4 of 12.8~ns (58\%) in wire delay, because in the
FP-enlarged fabric ($2.5\times$ the integer LUTs across eight wide FP lanes)
interconnect rather than logic depth has become the binding constraint.

\textbf{Step 2: confirming the diagnosis, 84~MHz.} If congestion is the limit,
de-congesting the fabric should raise frequency with the logic unchanged. The
eight per-lane divide/square-root cores are folded into a single shared serial
unit fed lane by lane. This removes about $3.7$k LUTs and $1.2$k flip-flops and
drops power from 1.53 to 1.22~W; crucially it leaves the FMA critical path
structurally unchanged yet cuts that path's routing delay from 7.4 to 6.5~ns,
raising the placed maximum to 84~MHz and reducing near-critical endpoints from
$8.7$k to $3.2$k. Same logic, less congestion, higher frequency: a direct
confirmation that interconnect, not logic depth, bounds this design.

\textbf{Localizing the limit: frequency does not scale with fabric size.} A
natural question is whether trimming the FP fabric relieves congestion further. It
does not. Placing the design at four lanes (Table~\ref{tab:fpclose}, DSE row)
halves its area, $36{,}785$ versus $68{,}914$ LUTs and 20 versus 40 DSP blocks,
but reaches only 79.7~MHz, marginally below the eight-lane 84.1~MHz, and the
router still reports CLB congestion. The critical path is a single lane's FMA
cone, whose logic depth and locally dense 128-bit datapath are independent of the
lane count; halving the lanes replicates that cone fewer times but packs the
smaller design into a denser region that gives the lone FMA no relief. The
eight-lane configuration therefore Pareto-dominates the four-lane one, at equal
frequency and twice the throughput, and identifies the true frequency lever as the
FMA microarchitecture rather than the fabric size.

\textbf{Step 3: closing 100~MHz with a three-stage FMA.} Acting on that finding,
the FMA is split across three stages---product, 128-bit align-and-add, and
normalize-and-round---so that no single stage carries the whole cone. The warp
pool absorbs the extra cycle with the same single-slot scoreboard, now a two-cycle
writeback latency that SIMT interleaving continues to hide on streaming work. The
added registers raise flip-flops to $14{,}954$ while leaving LUTs ($69{,}987$),
DSP blocks, power (1.22~W), and the bit-exact results unchanged. Place-and-route
now meets timing at 100~MHz with a worst setup slack of $+0.071$~ns, tracing
$78\rightarrow84\rightarrow100.7$~MHz across the three steps, and the critical
path moves off the FMA onto the vector-register-file LUTRAM read multiplexer, the
same structure that bounds the integer design (Section~\ref{sec:impl}). The
floating-point datapath is no longer the frequency limiter, which confirms that
FMA logic depth---the lever the design-space exploration identified---was the true
constraint.

\textbf{From out-of-context closure to a deployable SoC.} Closing the accelerator
out-of-context is not yet a chip. Integrated into \texttt{chip\_top} with the host
core and the eight-bank shared memory, the FP-enlarged SoC first places at only
82.5~MHz. The worst path is again congestion-bound, but now through the warp
scheduler's fetch--execute cloud: instruction fetch from the asynchronous LUTRAM
memory, decode, register-file read, execute, and writeback all completed in one
combinational cycle, and 82\% of the placed path was routing on its two
highest-fanout broadcast nets. Registering FP operands at issue, replicating the
broadcast nets, and fully pipelining the DSP multiplies recover part of the gap
(96.4~MHz) but leave the single-cycle cloud intact. The last of those changes also
deepens the FP unit: \texttt{fmul} and the FMA need the same 48-bit significand
product, so it is computed once on a \emph{shared} $24\times24$ DSP cascade
carrying a full input register and three output registers, the pattern that draws
no DSP pipelining advisories. That places the product four cycles after the
operands and makes the unit in the deployed chip five stages deep rather than
three; the stage-0 payload rides a matching delay line, the arithmetic is untouched
and every bit-exact result unchanged, and the single-slot \texttt{W\_FPC}
scoreboard counts out the longer latency. Splitting the fetch--execute cloud with a
fetch/execute pipeline register---the fetched instruction and its warp context are
registered at a new stage boundary, and a per-warp in-flight bit preserves the
scheduler's re-issue semantics exactly---applies the SIMT latency-hiding argument
at instruction grain: with two or more runnable warps the pipeline stays full, and
the measured cycle cost is at most 0.3\% on 1024-element kernels. The full chip
then places at 107.3~MHz unconstrained and, with every port pin-constrained for
the ZCU104 (clock on a clock-capable pin) and a warning-free design-rule check
with no waivers or severity downgrades, signs off at $+0.360$~ns worst setup slack
(103.7~MHz), $71{,}567$ LUTs (31\%), 40 DSP blocks, zero block RAM, and 1.20~W
(Table~\ref{tab:fpclose}, final row), yielding a deployable bitstream.

Open RISC-V GPGPU cores include Vortex~\cite{vortex}, a full OpenCL-capable
multi-core design, and Ventus~\cite{ventus}, a PTX/Vortex-style GPGPU; both target
performance and scale and are correspondingly large. Simty~\cite{simty}
demonstrates generalized SIMT execution of the RISC-V ISA, integer-only and
reporting synthesis area in logic elements on an Intel Cyclone~IV, and
Nyuzi (published as Nyami)~\cite{nyuzi} is a synthesizable vector GPGPU for
graphics research.

Table~\ref{tab:related} places SIMTiX next to the per-core FPGA numbers published
for Vortex, the closest comparable design: the complete SIMTiX SoC, host CPU,
driver ROM, and eight-bank shared memory included, fits in the logic budget of
roughly one Vortex core of equal warp and lane geometry. The frequency columns,
however, are not directly comparable, and we do not read the gap as a two-fold
speed deficit. The Vortex entries are pre-placement synthesis estimates on an
Intel fabric; the system actually demonstrated in~\cite{vortex} runs at 200~MHz.
The SIMTiX entries are post-route on UltraScale+ and are bounded by the 100~MHz
target we constrain the build to rather than by a swept maximum: both SoCs close
that target with positive slack, so the reported figure is a signed-off operating
point, not a ceiling. The wider point is that a pre-placement number says little
about the placed one: Section~\ref{sec:fppa} reports our FP design synthesizing to
a 5.3~ns logic depth yet closing at 12.8~ns placed, because the binding constraint
turned out to be routing congestion rather than logic depth. That placed,
signed-off closure study is not reported for the larger cores. Open floating-point units such as
FPnew~\cite{fpnew} offer multiformat arithmetic as standalone IP; SIMTiX instead
integrates a per-lane FP datapath into the SIMT pipeline and studies its placed
timing. Control-divergence handling via reconvergence stacks is long
studied~\cite{fung,tesla}; SIMTiX uses the standard stack mechanism and reuses its
mask for lane clock-gating.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================
SIMTiX shows that the full SIMT mechanism set fits in a compact RISC-V
accelerator that runs end-to-end through a real implementation flow, with a
distributed-RAM register file the key to making it small and fast on FPGA, and a
per-lane unit adding FP32 and FP16 arithmetic verified bit-exact over
$116{,}089$ vectors. The central result is a placed timing-closure study: the
FP-enlarged fabric is interconnect-bound, a limit diagnosed, confirmed by
de-congestion, and localized to a single lane's FMA cone, then closed at 100~MHz
with a three-stage FMA that moves the critical path onto the same register-file
read multiplexer that bounds the integer design. A fetch/execute pipeline in the
warp scheduler carries that closure to the complete SoC, which signs off at
103.7~MHz with full ZCU104 pin constraints and a warning-free design-rule check,
at a measured cycle cost under 0.3\%. An ASIC path through the SkyWater
130\,nm PDK~\cite{sky130} with OpenLane~\cite{openlane} would turn these FPGA
numbers into silicon-relevant area, frequency, and power.
